# Secondary Dominants

## Description

Secondary dominants are non-diatonic chords that create strong resolutions to chords other than the tonic (the I chord). They are one of the most common ways to add harmonic interest and complexity to a standard diatonic chord progression.

## Content

### What is a Dominant Chord?

First, let's recap the primary dominant. In any given key, the dominant chord is the V chord. For example, in the key of C Major, the V chord is G Major (or G7). The function of this dominant chord is to create tension that strongly resolves back to the tonic (I), in this case, C Major. The V-I resolution is the most powerful in Western music.

### The Concept of Secondary Dominants

A secondary dominant is a dominant chord that is the V chord of a diatonic chord *other than the tonic*. In simpler terms, we are "borrowing" the dominant chord from another key to temporarily make a different chord feel like the tonic.

Let's stay in the key of C Major. The diatonic chords are:
C (I), Dm (ii), Em (iii), F (IV), G (V), Am (vi), Bdim (vii°)

Now, let's say we want to resolve strongly to the Am (vi) chord. What is the V chord of A minor? It's E Major (or E7). So, E7 is the "secondary dominant" of Am. We write this as "V7/vi" (pronounced "five-seven of six").

We can do this for most of the diatonic chords in the key.

### Common Secondary Dominants (in the key of C Major)

-   **To the ii chord (Dm):** The V of Dm is A7. So, A7 is V7/ii.
    -   Progression example: C - **A7** - Dm - G
-   **To the iii chord (Em):** The V of Em is B7. So, B7 is V7/iii.
    -   Progression example: C - **B7** - Em - F
-   **To the IV chord (F):** The V of F is C7. So, C7 is V7/IV.
    -   Progression example: C - **C7** - F - G
-   **To the V chord (G):** The V of G is D7. So, D7 is V7/V.
    -   Progression example: C - **D7** - G - C
-   **To the vi chord (Am):** The V of Am is E7. So, E7 is V7/vi.
    -   Progression example: C - **E7** - Am - G

We typically don't create a secondary dominant for the vii° chord because it's already an unstable, dissonant chord.

### Why use them?

Secondary dominants add color and sophistication to your progressions. They create exciting moments of tension and release, leading the listener's ear in unexpected but satisfying directions. They are a cornerstone of jazz, pop, and classical music.
